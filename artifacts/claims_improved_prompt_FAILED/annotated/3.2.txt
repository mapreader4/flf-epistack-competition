⟦26⟧ This argument is considerably less bad than that given in the previous subsection. ⟦27⟧ Nonethe-
less it bears some resemblance to the space lizard argument, and observations seem to be
selected primarily on the basis of how much they stand out rather than any canonical or
complete collection of evidence. ⟦28⟧ This is fine if the observations really stand out - a factor of
50 is not bad, and you will be hard pressed to turn up many factors of 50 by chance. ⟦29⟧ But
Rootclaim includes five factors of 2 or less in their favor, and these should be plentiful to
find for any hypothesis, true or false. (⟦30⟧ Though Rootclaim did intend some of these factors
as being quite conservative.)
⟦31⟧ The numbers selected by Rootclaim also lead to some odd conclusions. For example, the
lack of known intermediate host is given more weight than the prior; ⟦32⟧ does Rootclaim believe
more than half of pandemics where we have failed to identify an intermediate host are lab
leaks? ⟦33⟧ Similarly, Rootclaim evaluates 80% of pandemics that first appear in Wuhan to be
lab leaks. ⟦34*⟧ It is a little difficult to understand what the "adjust for BSL-2" factor means in
isolation, but it seems to have the effect that half of all pandemics with highly contagious
viruses for which BSL-2 is inadequate would also be lab leaks.
⟦35⟧ Overall, I found Rootclaim's Bayesian calculation sufficient to be intriguing but not sufficient
to compel a conclusion.

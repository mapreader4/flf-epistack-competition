4.2  Applying model to zoonotic and lab-leak hypotheses

⟦1⟧ LetZ be the event that zoonotic spillover of covid took place at an east Asian wildlife market,
⟦2⟧ andLL be the event that covid resulted as a lab-leak from a lab conducting gain-of-function
research. ⟦3⟧ The observed index case is a shrimp vendor in HSM; call this observation H. We
want to estimate P (H|Z) and P (H|LL) using the model from the previous subsection. So,
for each hypothesis, how large of a circle do we have to draw around the primary case to
include the index case?
⟦4⟧ ForZ, the primary case would be any wildlife vendor at an east Asian market. We do not
have to draw a large circle to get from such a vendor to the index case; ⟦5⟧ the smallest circle
we could draw would include all residents at a market which has wildlife. 35 ⟦6⟧ At HSM, there
were 1162 vendors ([32] annex page 182), ⟦7⟧ which we round to 1200 as that makes the numbers
easier. (When including all employees and daily visitors I imagine it would go somewhat
higher than that.)
⟦8⟧ But HSM is not the only market, and there are potential primary cases at other markets,
so our circle must include all residents at those markets as well. In their written responses,

34This is a circle in epidemiology space, not physically on a map.
35By "resident" I mean not just vendors but any employee or daily visitor to the market; to a first

should draw a smaller circle that includes only half the market. Conversely, perhaps we should be including
household members of market vendors in our circle.
⟦9⟧ Saar gives the probability of the market in question being HSM as 0.2% [7], ⟦10⟧ and Peter gives
2% [8], ⟦11⟧ so by those numbers our circle should include 60 to 600 thousand people. 36
⟦12⟧ ForLL, the primary case would be any laboratory worker at a lab doing gain-of-function
research on coronaviruses. ⟦13⟧ In Wuhan, to draw a circle large enough to include the index case
requires including the whole city, as there is no known potential chain of transmission from a
lab worker to a resident at HSM that is significantly shorter than what you might expect to
any other person in the city. ⟦14⟧ Saar guesses that 20% of such GoF research is at WIV ([1] at
2:29:30), ⟦15⟧ so multiplying the population of Wuhan by 5 suggests a circle containing 60 million
people.
Our naive analysis then yields
⟦16⟧ P (H|Z) = 1/(6· 104) to 1/(6· 105)
⟦17⟧ P (H|LL) = 1/(6· 107)
⟦18⟧ for a Bayes factor of 100 to 1000 in favor of zoonotic spillover.
⟦19⟧ Many of the inaccuracies in our model will apply equally to both Z andLL, and thus cancel
out for the purposes of calculating a Bayes factor, but not all. We have supposed that
the contact graph is homogeneous, so that everyone has the same number of contacts; but
⟦20⟧ asymmetries in the graph could potentially cause a random walk to be more likely to end
up in some places than another. 37 ⟦21⟧ Saar claims that this effect causes outbreaks to be more
likely to show up at HSM than one might expect otherwise; if so, this raises the probability
of P (H|LL), but does not change P (H|Z) because under the zoonotic hypothesis our circle
is already been restricted to HSM.
⟦22⟧ We should also ask whether our analysis should be so sensitive to who the index case is:
⟦23⟧ the earliest cases were all close in time, and under a slight change in events the earliest
among them could easily have been someone else, plausibly even someone not at HSM.
⟦24⟧ We can address this by not considering a single index case but a sort of "average" of the
earliest cases; enough of them are HSM residents, or closely linked to HSM, that the result is
essentially the same as having a singular unambiguous index case who is a resident at HSM.
Had the earliest case data a more ambiguous nexus, this would have required more careful
analysis.
There are much more serious concerns with this analysis. ⟦25⟧ We have chosen Z to represent
zoonotic spillover at an east Asian wildlife market; if this feels arbitrary, that's because it
is. ⟦26⟧ Properly the debate concerns the possibility of zoonotic spillover at any location, not
necessarily a wildlife market and not necessarily in east Asia. ⟦27⟧ Notably, Saar observes [7] the
possibility of zoonotic spillover at a restaurant that processes live animals. ⟦28⟧ If we were to
expand Z to include restaurants and locations outside of east Asia, our circle would grow
enormously, and P (H|Z) be reduced in proportion. Conversely, since we are interested in
an outbreak that we know took place in Wuhan, we could reduce Z to zoonotic spillover at

HSM, or perhaps even down to a specific shop. ⟦29⟧ Each of these choices for Z is fully consistent
with the parameters of the debate; which choice of Z is "correct"?
⟦30⟧ Similarly, one could ask ifLL was chosen appropriately; would we be having the same debate
if covid appeared in a different city that lacked WIV but had other research laboratories

P (H|LL) = 1/(1.2· 107)
⟦32⟧ for a Bayes factor of 10000.
⟦33⟧ It is problematic for our estimates of P (H|Z) and P (H|LL) to vary by orders of magnitude
simply by taking a different perspective on the evidence: ⟦34⟧ the truth of the origin of covid
should not depend on the exact wording used by each side of the debate. ⟦35⟧ The resolution to
this problem is that as P (H|Z) varies wildly as we adjust our interpretation of the event
Z, the prior probability P (Z) varies wildly in the opposite direction. ⟦36⟧ If we broaden Z to
include all sorts of potential sources around the world, P (Z) increases; if we narrow Z to a
single specific resident at HSM, P (Z) decreases in accord. The joint probability P (H∩Z)
is in each case unchanged. 38
⟦37⟧ Therefore any discussion ofP (H|Z) andP (H|LL) is intrinsically linked to the more difficult
discussion of the prior probabilities P (Z) and P (LL). We will delay further analysis on this
to that time, in section 7.

4.1  A model for evaluating location of the index case

To estimate this, we will describe a crude model; while we do not know the quantitative
details of the close-contact graph, ⟦1⟧ fortunately our model is greatly insensitive to these details.
⟦2⟧ Suppose the shortest path from the primary case to the index case is n steps; call this the
epidemiological distance between them. Consider drawing a sphere around the primary case
that contains all people at most n steps away; certainly any random walk of length n that
starts from the primary case will end up at one of those people in the sphere, with some
nonzero probability. ⟦3⟧ Our simple model is to say that the index case follows a uniform
distribution within that sphere; that is, that the index case is equally likely to be any of the
people at most n steps from the primary case.
How good of a model is this? Well, let us consider two examples of simple graphs that might
be compared with the real-world close-contact graph.
⟦4⟧ A Bethe lattice of degree d is a tree 31 where each node has d neighbors; ⟦5⟧ Bethe lattices are
infinite dimensional.32 ⟦6⟧ If your social network were a Bethe lattice, then you would have 100
close contacts, and about 100 2 people within 2 steps, and 100 3 within 3 steps, and so on.
⟦7⟧ Random walks on a Bethe lattice very rapidly go away from the starting point; ⟦8⟧ after n steps
you will be a maximum of n steps away from where you started, ⟦9⟧ and on average be slightly
more than n(1- 2/d) steps away. ⟦10⟧ Our model that the random walk ends up uniformly
distributed within a sphere of radius n is very good on a Bethe lattice.
⟦11⟧ While the real-world close-contact graph does have loops (eg, especially within a household
or workplace) ⟦12⟧ respiratory diseases easily jump to strangers who will have few shared contacts.⟦13*⟧ 
On a very small scale, a Bethe lattice is a fair representation of the connectivity of close
contacts (this results in the "small-world phenomenon"). ⟦14⟧ One could also imagine that each
node on the Bethe lattice represents not a person but a small collection of closely connected
people, such as a household or workplace; ⟦15⟧ if so, when counting distances we would ignore
transmissions within the same group.
⟦16⟧ On a larger scale, around the size of a city, this Bethe lattice approximation breaks down,
⟦17⟧ because disease transmission is a physical process that requires proximity. ⟦18*⟧ As most indi-
viduals generally stay in roughly one area on the surface of the Earth during their illness,
